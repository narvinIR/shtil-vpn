Why banks and government sites break when the VPN is on

This is the single most common complaint about any VPN: you switch it on and your bank asks for extra confirmation, the government portal refuses you, the marketplace shows different prices. The cause is simple, and the fix is not «turn the VPN off».

What is actually happening

An ordinary VPN pushes every packet through the tunnel. A local site then sees a request from a foreign address and does exactly what it was built to do: raises its defences, demands extra confirmation, sometimes refuses outright.

This is not about VPNs being banned. To a bank, a login from another country is a sign of a stolen account, and reacting to it is its job.

How it gets solved

The route splits on the device itself: foreign addresses go through the encrypted tunnel, local ones go straight out as if the VPN were off. The site sees you at home, speed does not drop, extra checks never fire.

Shtil does this out of the box, but the technique is general — in clients built on the sing-box core it is called route splitting.

Two details everyone trips over

  • The lists of local domains and addresses must ship inside the app. Many clients fetch them at startup — and the server holding those lists is often unreachable from the very country that needs them, so the VPN never comes up at all.
  • For domains on the direct route, only the IPv4 address should be answered. Otherwise the device slips around the split via IPv6 and the site still sees a foreign visitor — the quietest failure of the lot.

How to check it

  1. Connect.
  2. Open a site that reports your public address — it should show a foreign one.
  3. Immediately open a local site that guesses your city — it should place you at home.
  4. If both say «abroad», the split is not working and everything is going through the tunnel.
